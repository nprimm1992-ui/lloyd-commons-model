  The new Portland International Airport main terminal, opened in 2024, has rapidly become the most photographed piece of civic

                                            The Pike Place Reference
 Seattle's Pike Place Market offers a powerful metaphor, for Lloyd Commons. It demonstrates the enduring value of a retained civic
   interior, continuously operated for nearly a century, evolving into the city's most recognizable "third place" and a major national
                                                           cultural destination.




Seattle cherishes Pike Place and Portland possessed a comparable civic asset in the Lloyd Center — now closed, still standing, and still
                                           available to be stewarded rather than demolished.




                                          The Architectural Lineage
ZGF Architects, the firm behind the new PDX terminal, is also the design firm for the approved Lloyd Center master plan. This shared
   lineage isn't the core argument. However, it serves as crucial evidence that the design capacity responsible for PDX's success is
                                    already embedded within the Lloyd Center's planning process.




   Lloyd Commons offers a structural approach that enables this design expertise to enhance an existing civic interior, rather than
                                    destroying one that completed a $50 million renovation in 2017.




                                                  The Civic Sequence
Just as PDX redefined the arrival experience, Lloyd Commons represents Portland's next opportunity to apply its design heritage to a
                                     building that has anchored the central city for seven decades.




    The Lloyd Center hosted a continuous calendar of civic and community programming — cultural festivals, local meetups, and
neighborhood events — through its closure. This activation record demonstrates that the building functioned as an operating public
                            realm, not merely a structure awaiting redevelopment. That record is the asset.




   Portland does not need to approve the demolition of the Lloyd Center to build a cultural
                       landmark. The landmark is already standing.

                              How This Gets Funded

 The Foundation acquires the site. The perimeter pays for the commons. The core is never
                                           sold.


                          Capital buys the asset. The asset yields forever. The yield sustains the mission.

The civic core is funded as mission capital — philanthropy, public partners, and impact investment — underwritten by fee title to 27
                                                                    acres.

The perimeter parcels are held in perpetuity by the trust and ground-leased. Rising land value stays with the community, and ground
                                                  rent funds the civic core's operation.

 A limited, time-boxed release of a minority of pads may fund the acquisition transition. This requires a supermajority vote with the
consent of easement holders of record. In the steady state the perimeter is ground-leased, not sold. The parcels are not a liquidation.
                                                They are an endowment that builds itself.

This is why the civic core can be preserved. The building is not asked to pay for itself. It is asked to keep doing what it already does —
                          bring people to the site every single day — while the land beneath it does the earning.

     What Lloyd Commons Is and Is Not




Lloyd Commons is an acquisition and stewardship model, not a land-use appeal. The master plan is settled law and nothing in this
 model asks any government to revisit it. The city's own findings confirm the plan is voluntary and the site can be redeveloped to
   similar capacity without it. The model works through a transaction: a civic buyer inside the seller's strategic review window.
