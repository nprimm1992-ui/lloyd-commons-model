The Moment
Lloyd Center closed permanently on August 8, 2026 — at roughly 90% vacancy, The building stands. The site is owned by KKR Real Estate Finance Trust
Inc. and Urban Renaissance Group, which hold an approved master plan for demolition and redevelopment into 14 parcels, approved by the Design
Commission on 5 March 2026 and upheld 10-0 by City Council in July 2026 after two days of testimony. Demolition is available as of right. The county
values the site parcels at roughly $57.6M; KREF carries them at $95.1M.


    The window in which a civic buyer can exist is open now.


This document describes what that buyer builds. The architecture below is deliberately vehicle-agnostic: it can be stood up as a new Foundation, adopted
by an existing Oregon nonprofit, or executed with a public partner — the structure holds regardless of which institution holds it.

The Core Acquisition Stack
Passion and pressure can open a door — but only a durable legal structure can keep it open. Each layer does a specific job that no other layer can do
alone.

This is how the legal entities nest inside each other to create both acquisition capacity and permanent mission protection. Each layer is essential —
remove one and the structure fails.




                                                                                                          Protected Core

This three-layer core is specified to the bylaws level in this model — an 11–15 member three-constituency board (community, professional, public
partner), supermajority triggers on all mission-affecting decisions, a bar on the Chair serving as Executive Director, and successor-operator provisions if
the operator ever fails or drifts.


    Every layer is load-bearing. Remove one and the structure fails.




         Fee disposition safeguard. Fee title to any perimeter parcel may be disposed of only by supermajority vote of the Foundation board together
         with the consent of easement holders of record. The default is retention. Disposition is the exception, and it is the hardest act the instrument
         permits. Perimeter parcels shall be ground-leased — ~70% in perpetuity, with up to ~30% released during the acquisition transition to fund the
         bridge to first rent. Retaining rather than selling the ~30% transition pads forgoes approximately $84.8M in pad-sale proceeds — the sold-pad
         band in the v3.3 model. That is the price of permanence, and it is paid deliberately.




         Complexity here is not bureaucracy. It is anti-capture engineering — each layer blocks a different failure mode.

Community Assembly — design principles. The Assembly will convene neighborhood residents, tenants, anchor-institution representatives, and coalition-
nominated community members on a structured cadence (quarterly minimum; monthly during major decisions). Its powers will include: nominating

The Acquisition Frame
The Foundation acquires the whole; the perimeter is its currency. One entity takes the entire site — 27.1 acres, 14 parcels, the approximately 1,472,000
SF gross (Multnomah County records) structure, the garages. The capital stack flows through it. It then releases pieces on its own schedule: early releases
capitalise formation, later releases fund the core. The core and common areas are never released.


Why the Core Cannot Be Carved Out First
The seller's foreclosed book is $207.2M land against $117.3M buildings — a core sale means selling the written-down part. A carve-out forfeits the 14-
parcel entitlement, turns one whole-asset resolution into a two-obligation negotiation, and removes the development partner's economics. The
Foundation must be the buyer of the whole to be the seller of the parts.


The Consequence
Developers buy from the Foundation, not from the original owner. The premium the activated core creates — the civic amenity value that makes
surrounding parcels more valuable — accrues to the commons rather than to the seller. The Foundation captures the uplift it creates.

Market Context and the Fee-Disposition Safeguard
Two facts that sharpen the structure's timing and durability.


   SB 1521 and the Perimeter Residual
   SB 1521 (2026) requires Portland-area jurisdictions to offset inclusionary-housing costs. This is a positive for perimeter residual land value — it
   reduces the developer's cost burden on affordable units, which improves the residual available to pay ground rent. The $55,000/pad assumption is
   a timing-dependent figure, not a false one: the Foundation is the only buyer positioned to hold through the trough and release into the rebound.
   Portland multifamily averaged $182,489/unit in Q2 2026, down 12.9% year-over-year, against construction costs carrying roughly an 18% premium.
   Cost/value inversion is the current condition. It is not the permanent one.



   The Fee-Disposition Safeguard — Restated
   Fee title disposal requires a supermajority of the Foundation board plus the consent of easement holders of record. Perpetuity is legally available in
   Oregon: ORS 130.193 stewardship trusts may be enforced beyond 90 years. A third-party recorded public-access easement and a historic
   conservation easement under ORS 271.715 do the durable work and survive the Foundation's failure. The retention boundary and the designation
   boundary are the same line: what is kept is what would be designated. Which fabric carries the significance — the 1960 plan and rink, or the 1991
   glazed enclosure — is a question for professional assessment, and the two are not mutually exclusive. The filed nomination (National Trust 11 Most
   Endangered, October 2026) frames the 1991 enclosure as the strongest surviving architectural argument, on the reasoning that a 1960-integrity
   claim would likely fail on its merits. The trust model's prior framing held the opposite — that the nomination must rest on the 1960 plan, with the
   glulam vault as a later, non-contributing layer. Both the cross plan and the enclosure survive inside the retained core, which is why retention is the
   precondition for any listing that gates HB 3190 and a Historic Tax Credit pathway. Which fabric carries the designation is an open item requiring a
   preservation professional's assessment before the nomination advances. The safeguard is not a policy preference — it is a recorded instrument
   that outlasts any board. A nomination to the National Trust's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places program was filed in October 2026. It is an
   advocacy action; it does not create a designation, an easement, or a tax-credit pathway.




        The Foundation is the only buyer who can hold through the trough. Every other buyer needs to sell into the current market. The Foundation
        sells when the market recovers — and keeps the land either way.


The register is stated by intent. The commons is never pledged. Security may be taken on perimeter interests only, and only with mandatory partial
release on every pad sale or ground lease, no cross-default into the commons, and no acceleration reaching commons assets. The prohibition protects
the commons from encumbrance — it is not a bar on the acquisition financing route.

How the Asset Is Acquired
The model does not assume the asset. It names the transaction.

The site is owned by KKR Real Estate Finance Trust Inc. and Urban Renaissance Group — an entitled joint venture holding an approved master plan for
demolition and redevelopment into 14 parcels. The owner's plan already divides the perimeter into exactly the parcel structure the ground-lease
architecture contemplates.

The question this proposal answers is not whether the perimeter becomes 14 parcels — it does, under the owner's own approved plan. The question is
whether those parcels are sold or ground-leased, and whether the value they generate stays in the community or leaves with the first buyer.

The Foundation-controlled Property LLC is the buyer of record. The Trust lock attaches at or before close. The civic core is retained under a recorded
public-access covenant: approximately 450,000 square feet of the mall's internal street network — a connected series of glass-capped, three-storey linear
atria carried on a colonnade at the storefront line, with the ice rink at the crossing, and the adjoining wings. The covenant protects the network, not a
single hall. The 450,000 SF boundary is provisional pending the core-boundary design decision; the covenant should be written to the network's
geometry, not to a square-footage count, so that a refined boundary does not require reopening the instrument. The perimeter is a negotiation over
disposition form. The core structure is the difference between a district that keeps its public interior and one that loses it. Tier 1 activation — restoring
public circulation through the core — is a reopening, not a construction. The interior already contains a connected, glass-capped, transit-linked
promenade network; the circulation, the light, and the volume exist. The trust's differentiator is not that it will build a civic interior — it is that it already
holds one. The trust retains ~70% of pads under perpetual ground lease. Up to ~30% may be released during the acquisition transition to fund the bridge
to first rent — permitted only by supermajority vote with easement-holder consent.

Retaining rather than selling the ~30% transition pads forgoes approximately $84.8M in pad-sale proceeds — the sold-pad band in the v3.3 model. That
is the price of permanence, and it is paid deliberately. In the steady state the perimeter is ground-leased, not sold.


The precedent mechanics are proven:

             Austin Community College / Highland Mall
             Austin, TX. Community college acquired a failing 1.2M-SF regional mall in phases (2010–2012, JCPenney parcel first at $15.7M, subsequent
             parcels through 2012). Retained the mall structure; transformed 200,000 SF into the Highland Campus, home to the ACCelerator learning
             lab. Its continued redevelopment was one project in ACC's $386M voter-approved, collegewide bond program (November 2014). 2026 ULI
             Americas Award for Excellence (Phase II, Perkins&Will + ERO Architects). Precedent for: phased public-benefit acquisition of a failed mall,
             delivering institutional recognition and sustained public value.



             Alexandria, VA — Industrial Development Authority
             The city's Industrial Development Authority purchased the 11-acre former Sears site for $54M and ground-leased it to Inova Health System
             for a term up to 99 years at $1/year — the public entity retaining fee title, the land serving a public purpose, and no subsidy consumed.



             Cortex, St. Louis
             A nonprofit master developer holding district-scale powers — eminent domain, tax abatement, and plan-approval authority.



             Pier 57, NYC
             A public-trust landlord running food hall, corporate anchor, and 2-acre public rooftop under one structure.



             East Portland Community Investment Trust
             Portland, OR. Since 2017, nearly 350 lower-middle-class Portland residents have invested $767,000 in Plaza 122 through Mercy Corps
             Northwest's CIT — currently owning ~36% of the 29,000-SF property. Investment minimum: $10/month (average ~$80/month). More than
             120 families have successfully cashed out stakes totaling $310K. Loss-protected via letter of credit from Northwest Bank. Model now being
             replicated to Omaha, Albany NY, Tulsa, and Dallas. The community-investment layer works here — at scale, in this city, right now.



             Albina Vision Trust
             Three miles away. The civic-acquisition model is already in motion in this city.

Coverage sensitivity: 1.20× is the minimum coverage a lender will accept. Conventional commercial banks require 1.25–1.35×; at 1.25× this facility is $5.1M short of the draw, and at 1.30× $10.3M
short. The facility therefore requires a mission lender at 1.20×, a reduced draw, a higher rent, or credit enhancement covering the gap. The existing 0.31% margin leaves no room for any of these.
